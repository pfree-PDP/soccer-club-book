## Stage 1 — Session 1 (Mar 29, 2026)

### Origin Story (Author's Words, Paraphrased)
The idea came from a conversation with a parent whose daughter was on a team. That parent had been pulled into the role of team manager and was trying to figure out how to organize the team. That moment crystallized the core insight: someone should write a practical how-to guide for people who find themselves suddenly responsible for making a team work.

### The Core Concept as Stated
The book starts from the most relatable entry point — a parent with a kid who wants to play, surrounded by other kids who want to play, and now unexpectedly tasked with building something out of it. From that starting point, the book scales up:
- One team becomes multiple teams.
- Multiple teams require a board of directors.
- The club becomes a real business.

### Structural Insight: Multiple Entry Points
The author's instinct is that the book is **not just for one type of reader**. It uses the parent-turned-team-manager as the opening entry point, but it is designed to serve multiple audiences at different stages of the journey. The author specifically mentioned:
- A parent who is also now on the board of directors trying to run the club at an organizational level.
- Each audience segment would have its own "starting point" chapter or section.

### Key Phrase to Preserve
> "From the parent's point of view it would be like a how-to guide. But that's just one of the entry points."

### Clarification on the Reader's Point of View (Author's Words, Paraphrased)
The primary reader is a **parent who is involved at some level in a soccer organization** — not a professional club administrator, not a paid director, not a career coach. A parent. Someone who has likely only ever experienced the sport from the sidelines or as a spectator-participant. They get pulled into a role — team manager, board member, committee chair — and they have almost no preparation for it.

The defining characteristic of this reader is **inexperience born from limited exposure**. They may have done this once or twice with their own kids. They may have only ever watched from the sidelines. The book exists because that person is woefully under-resourced and there is currently nothing practical written for them.

### Emerging Book Concept (Synthesis)
The book is written from the parent's point of view, but it is designed to travel with that parent as their role grows. It meets them where they are — overwhelmed, well-meaning, and figuring it out — and walks them through every stage of increasing responsibility: from organizing a single team, to managing multiple teams, to sitting on a board, to running a club as a real business.

The reader does not change — the *stage* they are at changes. That is a meaningful structural insight: this may be a **single-journey book** rather than a multi-audience book, because the same parent can travel through all of these stages over time.

## Core Insight #1: People Don't Know What They're Getting Into

The foundational problem this book solves is a **knowledge gap at the point of entry**. Most people who get involved in managing a team or joining a soccer club or organization simply do not know what they are walking into. There is no orientation, no manual, no warning label. They show up thinking they are helping organize a group of kids, and they quickly discover there is far more to it than that.

The specific dimension of this that catches people most off guard is the **business reality of running a team**. A team is not just a team — it is a small business. There are real expenses: hiring a coach, entering tournaments, managing registration, handling administration, tracking finances. Every team, no matter how informal it feels at the start, operates like a business in miniature. And as the organization grows — more teams, a board of directors, a formal club structure — the business complexity grows with it.

The core mistake, then, is not a single bad decision. It is walking in blind. People underestimate the scope, the responsibility, and the operational demands of what they have agreed to take on.

### Key Phrase to Preserve
> "I don't really think people know what they're getting into when they start up managing a team or getting involved with a soccer club or a soccer organization."

### Why This Matters for the Book
This insight does two things simultaneously. First, it validates the reader — it tells them their confusion and overwhelm is not a personal failure, it is a structural gap that nobody has filled. Second, it defines the book's entire purpose: to be the thing that did not exist when they needed it. The book is the orientation manual, the warning label, and the operating guide all in one. That is a strong, clear promise to make to a reader.

## Core Insight #2: Why Parents Say Yes — The Control Dynamic

Parents who get involved in running a team or organization are often motivated, at least in part, by a desire to have some influence over their child's experience. The author is careful to note this is not the same as being a control freak — but it is worth naming, because that desire for involvement can quietly become a controlling dynamic once they are actually in a leadership or management role.

This is a psychologically honest observation that most books in this space would never say out loud. It has real implications for the book:

- It explains *why* parents volunteer in the first place — they care deeply and want to shape the outcome for their kid.
- It explains *why* managing other parents is one of the hardest parts of running a team or club — because you are often dealing with people whose emotional investment outpaces their organizational experience.
- It creates a tension the book should address directly: the same motivation that makes someone a passionate volunteer can make them a difficult collaborator.

### Key Phrase to Preserve
> "I wouldn't go so far as to say they're control freaks, but often that becomes the case when you're having to deal with them."

### The Spectrum of Parent Motivation (Author's Elaboration)
The author identified two ends of the motivation spectrum:

**The good side:** "I want to create as positive an environment for my child as I can." The entry point is genuine care — my child is interested in this, and I want to contribute to make it the best experience possible.

**The problematic side:** A desire for control, a need to keep a close eye on their child, or anxiety — specifically, worry that their kid might not be good enough. That anxiety can drive over-involvement and make the parent difficult to work with in a shared leadership context.

This spectrum is important because the book's reader likely sits somewhere on it. Acknowledging both ends honestly — without judgment — will make the book feel credible and self-aware.

## Core Insight #3: The Hardest Transition — From Managing Teams to Coordinating Groups of Teams

The author identified the most difficult operational leap in their experience: moving from managing their own team (or two teams) to organizing and coordinating a group of 19 teams. This is a fundamentally different kind of work, and most people are not prepared for it.

When you manage your own team, you are the decision-maker. You know your players, your parents, your schedule. When you are coordinating a group of coaches and teams, you are now a layer removed from the action. You are managing people who are managing teams. The problems that surface are different:

- Coaches at **varying levels of experience and skill** — some capable, some not, and you are responsible for the whole group's output.
- Coaches who need **information** — and there is no system yet to distribute it consistently.
- Coaches who need help **registering for tournaments** — an administrative function that multiplies with every team you add.
- Coaches who need help **managing money** — budgets, fees, expenses, all at the team level, with no central infrastructure.

The shift is from doing to coordinating, from player-facing to coach-facing, from one team's problems to nineteen teams' problems arriving simultaneously.

### Why This Matters for the Book
This is one of the most important transitions the book must address — and one that is almost never written about. There is a gap between "I run a team" and "I run a club," and that gap is full of operational, communication, and leadership challenges that catch people completely off guard. The author lived it. That is the material.

## Core Insight #4: The Operational Reality of Scale

At the 19-team stage, the author and Dave were responsible for approximately **250–300 players**. The affiliated club handled much of the formal registration infrastructure, but everything else fell to them. The operational list was long and unforgiving:

- **Player registration** — all 250-300 players had to be registered and accounted for.
- **Field and practice space coordination** — finding, scheduling, and managing physical spaces for that many teams.
- **Team problems** — handling issues as they arose across 19 different teams simultaneously.
- **Coach attrition** — dealing with coaches who quit mid-season, one of the most disruptive operational problems a club can face.
- **Tryouts** — organizing the process of evaluating and placing players across multiple teams.
- **Season management** — the full cycle of filling a team roster and running a complete season, multiplied by 19.

The author's summary of what this required: **"Dave and I had to coach everybody on" everything.** They were not just administrators — they were also the support system, the training resource, and the problem-solvers for every coach in the group. There was no manual. They were building the plane while flying it.

### Key Phrase to Preserve
> "Dave and I had to coach everybody on."

### Why This Matters for the Book
This passage is a goldmine of chapter material. Every item on that operational list is a topic the book needs to address: registration systems, field logistics, coach recruitment and retention, tryout processes, season planning. And the meta-lesson underneath all of it is that **at scale, your job changes from doing the work to building the systems that let others do the work.** That is a teachable concept that belongs early in the book's scaling section.

## Core Insight #6: The Culture and Alignment Problem at Scale (Incomplete — Author Trailed Off)

One of the central challenges at scale is **alignment of vision** and **connection between teams**. When you are running 19, 20, 30, or 40 teams entrepreneurially — meaning each team is essentially operating independently — it becomes impossible to create a shared culture or consistent organizational structure. Each individual team remains its own isolated experience.

But when you are running as one of the top clubs in the country, the stakes are fundamentally different. The fees go up. Parent expectations shift dramatically — from *my kid is having fun playing soccer* to *my kid needs to be seen by college coaches, professional coaches, and may have a career in this sport.* As the professionalism of the club ratchets up, so does the expectation, the cost, and the personal investment of every family involved.

This creates a compounding pressure on the organization. You are no longer just managing operations — you are managing the hopes and financial commitments of families who are paying serious money for a serious outcome. That changes everything: how you communicate, how you make decisions, how you handle conflict, what your coaching standards need to be, and what your accountability structures look like.

### Key Phrase to Preserve
> "As the professionalism of the club ratchets up, so does the expectation and the cost and the investment."

### Why This Is Important
This is the insight that separates a collection of teams from a real club — and separates a recreational club from an elite one. The entrepreneurial, each-team-for-itself model works at small scale but breaks down as the organization grows and the stakes rise. At some point, culture, standards, shared identity, and a clear value proposition have to be deliberately built. They do not emerge on their own.

This also reframes the parent dynamic introduced earlier. The parent who got involved to help their kid have a good experience is now a parent who is paying thousands of dollars a year and expecting a pathway to college or professional play. That is a very different relationship to manage. The book needs to address how clubs navigate this transition — and how to set and manage expectations honestly at every level.

## Core Insight #7: Club 2.0 — What Was Broken and What They Built

### The Problem Before Club 2.0
Prior to the restructuring, the club's operational model was fragile and unsustainable:

- The entire business was effectively **run by two people** — a club administrator and a director of coaching.
- The support staff structure was **volunteer-driven and chaotic** — high turnover, no consistency, no systems.
- Staff people were constantly turning over, which meant institutional knowledge walked out the door repeatedly.

This is a recognizable pattern in growing clubs: the organization outgrows its original structure but has not yet built the infrastructure to replace it. The result is a fragile operation held together by a small number of overloaded individuals.

### The First Attempt: Hiring an Executive Director
In the author's first three years on the board, the club hired an **executive director** to head the administrative function. This was a meaningful step — an acknowledgment that the club had grown beyond what volunteers alone could manage.

The result was mixed. The hire worked reasonably well, but the person did not have all the skills the full job required. This led to a key insight:

> **"The full job of an executive director is very complex, and finding a person like that is kind of like finding a unicorn."**

This is one of the most practically useful observations in the book. The executive director role at a mid-to-large soccer club requires a rare combination: administrative competence, financial literacy, people management, event coordination, parent communication, coaching program oversight, and strategic thinking. Almost no single hire will cover all of it. Clubs that expect one person to do everything will be disappointed repeatedly.

### Club 2.0: Timeline and Context (Corrected)
Club 2.0 came **after COVID** and after a **major board restructure**. The sequence matters:

1. COVID hit and nearly destroyed the club financially.
2. The club survived COVID (partly through government funding programs).
3. A major board restructure followed — rebuilding the governance model.
4. Club 2.0 emerged from that restructure as a deliberate initiative to establish **organizational and operational efficiencies**.

The focus areas of Club 2.0 included:
- **Financial management** — building real financial infrastructure and oversight.
- **Operational infrastructure** — the systems and processes necessary to keep the club functioning as a viable business.
- The broader goal: ensuring the club could **sustain itself** — not just survive a crisis, but operate with enough structure that it would not be perpetually one bad year away from collapse.

This was not a growth initiative. It was a **stabilization and professionalization** initiative. The club had to earn the right to grow again by first getting its house in order.

### The Financial Bridge: Government Funding Programs
During COVID, the club accessed a variety of government financial assistance programs. These became a **critical revenue source** — not just a lifeline during the crisis, but the financial foundation that enabled the transition into Club 2.0. Specifically, the funding allowed the club to:

- Survive the period when no programs were running and registration revenue had stopped.
- Hire **additional staff** to begin professionalizing the operation.
- Move from a reactive, crisis-driven posture into a deliberate restructuring mode.

The author was directly involved in identifying and securing this funding. This is a concrete, practical skill that the book should address: **knowing what financial resources exist for nonprofit and community sports organizations, and how to access them.** Most club leaders have no idea these programs exist until they are already in crisis.

### Elite Programming and Club 2.0 Formalization
Around the same time as COVID and the Club 2.0 transition, the club launched its **elite boys programming** and began building out the **girls programming**. Club 2.0 was the operational context that made this possible: it was during this phase that the club **formalized the roles** needed to lead those programs and **figured out how to pay for it all**.

This is an important sequencing lesson for the book: you cannot build elite programming on an unstable operational foundation. The infrastructure has to come first. Club 2.0 was the infrastructure. The elite programs were what the infrastructure enabled.

### Club 3.0: What Comes Next
At the tail end of the Club 2.0 period, the board is now structuring itself around **Club 3.0**. The shift in focus is significant:

| Phase | Focus |
|---|---|
| Club 2.0 | Standardizing — operational efficiency, financial infrastructure, formalizing roles |
| Club 3.0 | Optimizing — increasing investment, deepening professionalism, building culture, coach cohesion |

The language the author used is precise and worth preserving:
> **"Going from standardizing to optimizing inside the business."**

This is a mature organizational framework. It names something that most clubs never articulate: there is a difference between getting your systems in place (standardizing) and making those systems excellent (optimizing). Most clubs either never finish standardizing, or they skip it entirely and try to optimize chaos. The ones that get it right do it in order.

### What Standardizing Actually Looked Like in Practice
Club 2.0 moved the organization from **chaotic and reactive** to **standardized and proactive**. The specific work this involved:

- **Coach pay levels** — establishing consistent, fair compensation structures for coaching staff.
- **Salary levels** — formalizing what roles were paid, at what rates, and on what basis.
- **Organizational infrastructure** — creating the structural framework that defined who was responsible for what.
- **Cash management and cash flow** — building systems to understand and control the flow of money through the organization.
- **Accounting** — moving from informal financial tracking to real accounting practices.

The author was **directly responsible for all the money** during this period. The recognition that drove the Club 2.0 initiative was blunt and honest:

> **"The business really needed someone who knew how to run a business to take a pass at it."**

The author and Sherlyn Voltes positioned themselves to do exactly that — to bring genuine business administration expertise to an organization that had been running on goodwill, improvisation, and the heroic effort of a small number of overloaded volunteers. Their goal was to **professionalize the business administration infrastructure** of the club from the ground up.

This is the moment the club stopped being a community organization that happened to handle money and started being a real business that happened to be a community organization. That distinction matters enormously.

### Why This Matters for the Book
This section covers some of the most under-discussed topics in club management: how to staff a growing organization, how to sequence growth and infrastructure, and the difference between surviving, standardizing, and optimizing. The unicorn problem — expecting one hire to do everything — is a trap that almost every club falls into at least once. And the 1.0 → 2.0 → 3.0 framework may itself be a useful organizing concept for the book's later chapters.

## Core Insight #8: The Financial Danger of Entrepreneurial Independence

The author zoomed out on the financial crisis question to make a broader structural point. Without going into the specific details of what happened at Santa Barbara Soccer Club, the pattern they are describing is universal:

When a club is running as a collection of **independent entrepreneurial units** — each team or program operating with its own vested interests and personal agendas — financial discipline breaks down. The specific failure modes:

- **Overspending** — individual teams or programs spend beyond their means because no one is watching the aggregate impact.
- **Budget blindness** — people do not recognize when they are operating outside of their budget parameters, because no clear parameters exist or are enforced.
- **Untracked decisions** — losing a team (and its registration revenue) or adding an unbudgeted staff hire can each have a significant financial impact. When no one is tracking the cumulative effect of these decisions, the organization can move from healthy to insolvent faster than anyone realizes.
- **Cash flow problems** — even a club that is technically profitable on paper can run out of operating cash if the timing of income and expenses is not actively managed.

### Key Phrase to Preserve
> "If you don't have somebody paying attention to the impact of, say, losing a team or making an investment in a new staff person that was not budgeted for, you can very quickly run out of money or get yourself into cash flow problems."

### Why This Matters for the Book
This is the financial chapter the book must have. Most club leaders think about money in terms of whether they have enough. The more sophisticated question — the one that separates clubs that survive from clubs that do not — is whether someone is actively watching the **flow** of money, the **impact** of individual decisions on the whole, and the **gap** between what was planned and what is actually happening. That requires a specific person, a specific role, and a specific set of habits. The book should name all three.

## Core Insight #9: The Episodic Board Problem

Board members in youth sports clubs tend to be **episodic** — they come in during the period when their child is involved, serve for a season or a few years, and then leave when their kid ages out or moves on. Each new board member arrives with their own ideas, intentions, and priorities. And they typically arrive with **no institutional knowledge** of what came before them.

This creates a structural problem that compounds over time. The author drew a direct analogy to soccer to explain it:

> **"Communication in the club is like communication on a soccer team. You rely on the knowledge you have of the other players and the playing style of the team in order to make decisions quicker and to execute decisions on the pitch better. Same is true in a board. If you have constantly changing people with their own ideas and intentions, you have a very challenging time creating a cohesive structure — and you can lose the ball more easily and get defeated."**

The analogy is precise. A soccer team that is constantly rotating players never develops the shared instincts, communication patterns, and trust that allow a team to execute under pressure. A board that is constantly turning over faces the same problem: every new member resets the learning curve, reintroduces old debates, and dilutes the institutional memory that makes good governance possible.

### The Specific Failure Modes
- **No institutional memory** — decisions made years ago, and the reasons behind them, are lost when the people who made them leave.
- **Repeated mistakes** — without memory, the organization relitigates the same issues and makes the same errors across board cycles.
- **Misaligned priorities** — each new board member brings their own agenda, which may conflict with the direction the club has been building toward.
- **Slow execution** — a board that does not know each other well cannot move quickly or confidently, especially under pressure.

### Why This Matters for the Book
This is a governance chapter that most club handbooks never address. The book should offer practical guidance on how to manage board continuity: onboarding processes, documentation of decisions and rationale, term structures that ensure overlap between experienced and new members, and the cultural practices that build shared understanding over time. The soccer analogy is a gift — it makes an abstract governance problem feel immediately concrete to the book's audience.

### Critical Structural Note: Handbook, Not Memoir
The author is explicit: this is **not a memoir**. The personal journey is a thread, not the subject. The intended structure is something like:

> *Here is what was happening with me at this stage — and here is what I learned from it, and what you should do.*

The author's story serves as a **through line and credibility anchor**, not the main event. Each stage of their experience introduces a set of lessons that are then translated into practical guidance for the reader. The personal narrative earns the reader's trust; the handbook content is what they came for.
