# Author Style Guide: The Accidental Club Leader

**Status:** Active project guide  
**Last Updated:** 2026-07-27  
**Purpose:** Governs the voice, structure, and editorial craft for drafting the book.

This document governs the voice, structure, and editorial craft for drafting the book. It translates Paul’s natural speaking style into a written standard that balances humor, hard-won experience, and serious operating systems, without drifting into academic or consulting territory.

---

## 1. The Core Voice

The book should sound spoken, not presented. The reader should feel like they are sitting next to an experienced guide who has already survived this particular mess and is explaining how it works.

- **Conversational and observant:** Write directly to another parent, coach, team manager, or club leader.
- **Self-aware:** Be willing to laugh at the situation and at the author’s own mistakes.
- **Direct when it matters:** Become entirely serious when discussing systems, safety, money, governance, or adult behavior that harms players.
- **Experienced, not omniscient:** The author has encountered problems the reader may face now or later. The author is ahead in experience, not above the reader.

*Test:* Would Paul actually say this out loud to another coach over coffee, on the sideline, or after a board meeting?

## 2. Use Normal Language Before Organizational Language

Paul’s ordinary language is part of the book’s identity. Do not polish it into generic business jargon.

**Keep phrases like:**
- “The coach handles the soccer. The manager handles the stuff.”
- “Players play. Coaches coach. Parents cheer.”
- “Good assistants challenge privately and support publicly.”
- “You can delegate the work, but not the relationships.”
- “At some point, somebody has to own it.”
- “Good intentions only get you so far.”
- “What works for one team may not work for a whole club.”

**Avoid imported consulting language:**
- *Instead of:* “The player is the moral and decision-making center of the organization.”
- *Use:* “The players are the reason we’re doing all of this in the first place. That doesn’t mean every decision is about playing time. Sometimes the decision is about money or registration, but eventually all of that affects the players.”
- *Instead of:* “As the scope of responsibility expands, informal effort must become roles, systems, governance, and culture.”
- *Use:* “What works when you have one team usually stops working when you have five teams. You can’t keep everything in one person’s head. People need clear jobs, and the club needs a way to keep running when one volunteer steps away.”

## 3. The Function of Humor

Humor is seasoning, not a comedy routine. It should arise from recognizing how youth soccer actually works—the absurdity of running a volunteer organization, the gap between what adults say and how they behave, and the difference between the plan and reality.

- **Keep funny lines short:** Do not explain the joke. Let it land, then move back to the serious point.
  > *“Our communication system was very simple. Everyone texted me separately.”*
- **Target the situation, not the people:** The humor should make the reader nod in recognition, not make players, parents, or volunteers feel mocked.
- **Use self-deprecation carefully:** Admit when Paul was wrong, cared too much about winning, or created a broken system. But do not make him sound incompetent. The tone is: *“I was capable, committed, and sometimes very wrong.”*

## 4. The Narrative Movement

Do not leave the reader stuck in a story, and do not present an abstract leadership framework before the reader understands the problem. The personal narrative is the delivery mechanism; the reader’s development is the purpose.

**A standard chapter movement:**
1. **The Story:** Here’s what happened. (Specific, personal, slightly wandering like a real memory.)
2. **The Illusion:** Here’s what I thought was happening.
3. **The Reality:** Here’s what was actually happening (what I got wrong).
4. **The Lesson:** Here’s what I learned.
5. **The Practice:** Here’s what you may want to do before it happens to you.

## 5. Structuring the Lesson

The chapters should be organized, but the reader should not constantly see the machinery.

- **Do not make every lesson sound profound.** Some lessons are big statements about leadership. Some are simply: *“Make sure two people know where the player cards are.”* Practical lessons build credibility.
- **Let the principle emerge from the story.**
  > *Instead of:* “Leadership includes direction, accountability, boundaries, systems, and protection.”
  > *Use:* “At first, I thought leadership mostly meant knowing what to do and getting people to follow you. Later, I realized a lot of it was less glamorous. It was deciding who was responsible, following up when something didn’t happen, and sometimes saying no to a person you actually liked.”
- **Keep operating material connected to real problems.** Do not introduce a chapter as being about “organizational sustainability.” Instead, say: *“A club can get pretty far on a few good people working really hard. The problem is that eventually those people get tired, move on, or realize nobody else knows how anything works.”*

## 6. Managing the “North Star” Principles

The book relies on recurring truths that condense judgment. They should emerge naturally from stories and decisions, not appear as branded slogans in every chapter.

**The core inventory:**
- Remember why you said yes.
- Passion isn’t the problem. Direction is.
- The coach handles the soccer. The manager handles the stuff.
- We play to win. We train to grow the player.
- Delegate responsibilities. Never delegate relationships.
- Every adult exists to create an environment where players can improve themselves.
- The player does the doing.
- Every role should make player development easier, not harder.
- Protect the environment, not your ego.
- Same responsibilities. Different organization.
- Helping one player eventually becomes helping hundreds.

*Rule:* Use these sparingly. They should feel like earned conclusions, usually at turning points or chapter endings.

## 7. The Three Editing Tests

Before finalizing any section, pass it through these three filters:

1. **The Spoken Test:** Would Paul actually say this out loud to another coach over coffee, on the sideline, or after a board meeting? If not, simplify it.
2. **The Reality Test:** Does this sound like someone explaining what really happens, or someone presenting a leadership framework? Readers should feel like they are being walked through something learned the hard way.
3. **The Boardroom Test:** When the topic becomes serious (money, safety, governance), is the language clear and specific enough that people know exactly what must happen next?
